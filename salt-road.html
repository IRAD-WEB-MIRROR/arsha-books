Western Ottuk Library No. 2

The Salt Road

Tuz jolu

by Ormon Satarov · translated from the Iradi by Aidana Mukash kyzy

November 1921, the first winter of the hunger. The clans of the upper Chatkal have no salt, and the sheep are licking the walls. So the elders give Orozbai Myrzakul uulu, a Red cavalry scout with a crippled hand, the women’s silver and a boy of sixteen, and send him down over the passes to find some.

There is salt in Aulie-Ata, a warehouse full of it, and it belongs to a speculator with a paper and a purple stamp. There is salt on Kulan-Sor, a white pan at the end of the steppe, and it belongs, the old salt-cutters say, to the Rider, who gives it away. Between the pan and the Chatkal are three hundred miles, a food commissar who believes in stamps, a Cossack who never hurries, and two passes closing in the snow. To carry the salt home Orozbai needs twelve camels, and the camels come with Satkyn, who thinks very little of mountain men.

Published in Iradi in 1962 and cut by the censor, The Salt Road was No. 2 in the Ottuk Library, from the series’ first year, and Ormon Satarov’s first novel. This first English translation restores the passages that were cut, including the rider at the top of the last pass.

Chapter One

The whole first chapter, including the passage the censor cut in 1962.

By the time Orozbai Myrzakul uulu came out with the milk pail, the ewe had been licking the wall of the yurt since first light, and her tongue was bleeding.

She stood with her forelegs braced against the lattice and worked at the felt as if there were something in it she had lost. The felt was old, and grey with smoke, and it had the sweat of three generations of his family in it, and it was the nearest thing to salt she could find. Orozbai set the pail down and took her by the wool of her neck and pulled her off. She came away with a long string of red spit hanging from her mouth, and stood looking at him, and then went straight back to the wall.

He let her. There was no point in anything else.

All along the row of winter yurts it was the same. The sheep licked the felts and the stones of the hearths and the frozen ground where the women threw out the ash. The goats had stripped the bark from the willows by the stream. In the horse lines the mares were chewing their tethers, and the night before, a bay gelding of his uncle’s had eaten half a saddle girth and most of a bridle before anyone heard him. Orozbai had seen that once before, in the Fergana, in the second year of the war, when the remounts went three weeks without salt on the long road down from Namangan. He had seen the horses chew the leather off each other’s halters, and then gnaw the bones of a dead mule beside the road, cracking them in their big square teeth, which a horse does not do unless something inside it has gone very wrong. The army vet had told him why. Salt was what the blood was made of, and a body without it went looking for it everywhere, in leather and bone and earth and other horses’ sweat. A sheep was the same. A man was the same.

It was the eighth of November, 1921. The clans of the upper Chatkal had been out of salt since the end of summer, and the winter had not yet properly begun.

⁂

The council met in Odisho Jumabek uulu’s yurt after the morning milking, eleven men on the felts with their boots off and the smoke going up through the roof ring into a sky as hard and pale as ice on a trough. Orozbai sat near the door, where the young men sat. He was twenty-two. He had been home a month.

He had come back from the Fergana in October with a hole through the back of his left hand, two fingers that would not close and a paper that said the Red Army had no further use for him.

Now he was home, and the sheep were eating the walls.

Odisho had the salt chest brought in and set it in the middle of the felts, and opened it, and let them look.

It was a good chest, of walnut, carved with rams’ horns and crosses, black with age where hands had held it. It had stood on the right side of Odisho’s hearth for as long as anyone in the camp could remember, and the salt of the whole clan had been kept in it, bought in the autumn at the Fergana bazaars and carried up over the passes on camels and given out by the elder’s wife a cupful at a time through the winter. Now there was a little grey dust in the corners of it, and nothing else.

“I have been in the Chatkal sixty-seven years,” Odisho said. “I have seen hungry winters. I have not seen this.”

Nobody answered him. There was nothing to say that every man there did not know. The war had closed the Fergana bazaars, and a trader with twelve camels on those roads was a man everybody wanted to shoot. The two men the clan had sent down to Talas in September with a string of horses to sell had come back with the horses and a story about a food committee and a price that nobody believed.

“Without salt we lose the flocks by spring,” said Odisho. “Not all. Enough. The milk is already going thin. The women cannot make kurut.” He meant the hard dried curd that kept a camp alive through the end of winter. “Without salt it rots. The meat we kill in the cold will not keep past the thaw. So by the spring we have no curd, no meat and half the sheep. And then it is not the sheep who are hungry.”

He was a big old man, with a white beard cut square and hands that had broken horses for half a century, and he did not say things twice. He let it sit. Then he turned and looked down the felts to where Orozbai sat by the door.

“Myrzakul’s son,” he said. “You have been to the edge of the world and back. Where is there salt that nobody has shut?”

Orozbai had known for a week that somebody would ask him, and had known for a week what he would say. He said it.

“Kulan-Sor.”

There was a stir among the older men. One of them laughed, without much in it.

“Kulan-Sor is at the end of the earth,” said his uncle Joldoshbek. “It is past Aulie-Ata. It is past the Karatau. It is in the desert, among the Eski El, who are not even Christians, whatever they say.”

“It is three hundred miles,” said Orozbai. “Perhaps a little less. I met a man in the army from Tuzduu, which is the village by the pan. He said the salt lies on the ground there like snow, fourteen miles of it, and in autumn you can cut it with a spade. He said nobody owns it.”

“Somebody always owns it,” said Joldoshbek.

“He said the Eski El say it belongs to the White Rider.”

That got a silence. A man by the hearth crossed himself. Odisho looked at him with his old pale eyes, and Orozbai looked back, and did not let himself look away, though he wanted to.

“Two passes there,” Odisho said at last. “Two passes back. With camels, if the Eski El will give you camels. In winter.”

“Not winter yet,” said Orozbai. “If I go tomorrow, I am at the pan in ten days. Fifteen days back with loaded camels, perhaps eighteen. I am over the Kara-Buura before the big snow.”

“Perhaps.”

“Perhaps,” Orozbai agreed.

Odisho nodded slowly, as if Orozbai had answered something other than what he had asked. Then he called to his wife behind the curtain, and after a while she came out with a white felt folded over her arms, and knelt and laid it open on the floor beside the empty chest.

On the felt was the silver of the camp.

Orozbai had not seen it all together before. Nobody had. It was the women’s silver, the coins from the ornaments of their elechek headdresses, which a married woman wore wound high and white about her head with silver hung at the temples, and the bracelets and rings and the heavy old pendants that came down from grandmother to granddaughter at weddings. There were Bukhara coins and Kokand coins and Russian roubles of the old Tsar, and two broad bracelets set with red stones that had belonged to Odisho’s mother, and a child’s silver cross on a thread. The women had taken them off the night before and given them to Odisho’s wife without a word, she said, the way they would have given milk.

“For the camels,” said Odisho. “And for the salt, if they will sell it.”

Orozbai looked at the silver lying on the white felt, and at the empty walnut chest beside it.

⁂

His cousin Addai found him at the horse lines in the dusk, rubbing down the army gelding with a twist of hay.

The gelding was a Chongtor, mouse-dun, with a black stripe down his back from the withers to the tail and the remount brand on his near shoulder, a short-legged, deep-bodied horse with a head like a hammer. Orozbai had never named him. In the army nobody named the remounts, because they died too fast. While Orozbai worked at his flank he kept swinging his head round to lick the left hand, the bad one, for the sweat in the palm.

“Take me,” Addai said.

He was sixteen, Baitik’s youngest, all elbows, good with horses and with nothing else Orozbai had seen, least of all keeping his mouth shut.

“No.”

“You need someone for the spare horses. You can’t hold a lead rope with that hand. I’ve seen you try.”

That was true, and Orozbai said nothing.

“I asked my father,” said Addai. “He said ask you. Odisho said ask you.” He waited, and when nothing came, he said, more quietly, “I want to see something. Once. Before I’m old.”

Orozbai stopped rubbing and stood with the hay in his good hand and looked at the boy in the dusk. He had said something very like that to his own father in the spring of 1919. Then the gelding pushed its nose into his bad hand again, looking for salt, and he thought about the ewe with her bleeding tongue.

“Six spare horses,” he said. “You lead them, you water them, you feed them, you sleep with them. If you talk on the road I’ll send you home from Talas.”

Addai’s face split open in the dark.

“I won’t talk,” he said.

⁂

They left an hour before dawn on the ninth, with six spare horses on a lead behind Addai and the silver sewn into the lining of Orozbai’s saddle under a fold of felt, and the whole camp standing in the cold to see them go. Odisho’s wife came out with a bowl of milk and walked a little way down the path ahead of the horses, flicking the milk onto the frozen ground with her fingers, for the road. The old women stood together in their white headdresses with no silver on them now, so that they looked like a row of birches after the leaves have gone.

Orozbai rode at the head on the dun, with his Arisaka rifle across the saddle in front of him. It was a Japanese rifle, long and plain, of a kind the Red Army in Turkestan had been given in quantity and had never much liked, and when he was discharged the quartermaster had let him keep it with forty rounds, because, he said, there was no point in sending a man home to the Chatkal without one. Orozbai had cleaned it three times in the last week. He did not know who he expected to shoot.

They climbed. The track to the Kara-Buura went up the side valley behind the camp, along the stream and then out of the trees onto the open slopes, where the grass lay flat and brown under a skin of frost. By sunrise they were above the last of the juniper. By noon they were in the snow.

It was early snow, not deep, a hand’s breadth on the track and more in the hollows, dry and squeaking under the hooves, with a hard blue sky over it and no wind. Orozbai knew that kind of snow. It was the kind that came first and went away again and made men think the passes were still open. The real snow came after it, out of the west, with a wind behind it, and when it came the Kara-Buura shut like a door for five months and anybody on the wrong side of it stayed there.

Behind him Addai kept the spare horses in a tight string and did not talk, though Orozbai could feel how much he wanted to, like heat off a stove.

They came to the top of the Kara-Buura in the middle of the afternoon. The pass was a wide notch of broken stone with a cairn on it, hung with rags and horsehair, and from the top Orozbai could see north down the long empty trough of the upper Kara-Buura valley to the next pass, the Karakoyun, and the Talas beyond it, and beyond the Talas the whole wide world in which there was supposed to be salt.

He did not stop long. The wind was beginning on the top, and the sun was already going over to the west. He put a stone on the cairn for the road, as his grandfather had taught him, and told Addai to do the same, and Addai did it with great seriousness, and they started down.

⁂

They camped that night below the north side of the pass, at the head of the upper Kara-Buura valley, in the shelter of a rock where herders had camped before them. There was dung for the fire and a little dry grass for the horses under the snow. Orozbai unsaddled and hobbled the dun, and the dun stood looking at him with his ears pricked in the firelight, and then put his head down and began to lick the ground.

Orozbai went to see what he was licking.

It was nothing. A patch of trodden earth beside the track, a few yards across, where the snow had melted off in the sun and frozen again. The spare horses had found it too. All six of them had crowded onto it and were licking at the cold ground with their long tongues, shoving one another, and Addai was trying to pull them off and getting nowhere.

“Leave them,” said Orozbai.

“What is it? There’s nothing there.”

Orozbai crouched and put his good hand flat on the ground among the horses’ legs and then put his fingers to his mouth.

He knew then where they were. He had not known it was here. He had been in the Fergana when it happened, and had only heard about it afterwards, from his mother, in the spring, in a letter written by the priest that took two months to come.

⁂

It had been in August of 1920, in the time of the requisitions. A food detachment had come up from Talas over the Karakoyun, twelve soldiers with a commissar and a cart, with an order from Tashkent for grain and fodder for the army and the hungry cities. The Chatkal had no grain to speak of. They took the barley that was in the camp and the hay that was cut, and then the commissar, who was a young man in a leather coat who did not speak Iradi, saw Odisho’s walnut chest by the hearth and asked what was in it, and when he was told, he had it emptied into sacks.

Odisho had tried to tell him that there was no salt nearer than the Fergana, that the clan could live without barley but not without salt, that the sheep would die. The commissar had listened politely through his interpreter. Then he had written out a receipt for the salt, at the state price, and given it to Odisho, and they had gone back up over the Kara-Buura with the cart.

The cart had broken an axle just here, on the north side of the pass, in the first rain. It was too heavy to take on over the Karakoyun on one wheel, and the soldiers had to lighten it, and salt was the heaviest thing on it that a soldier could not eat. So they had slit the sacks with their bayonets and poured the salt of the upper Chatkal out into the mud beside the track, and gone on with the barley.

The herders had found the place a week later. The salt was mostly gone into the ground by then, in the rain. The old women went up anyway, Orozbai’s mother among them, two days’ walk, and knelt in the mud on the north side of the Kara-Buura and scraped up what they could with spoons, and carried it home in their headcloths. It came to about a cupful for every family, grey with dirt. They put it in the walnut chest. It lasted until the spring.

Odisho still had the receipt. He kept it in the empty chest.

⁂

Orozbai stood up. The horses were still licking at the ground, all eight of them now, the dun among them, in the firelight. Fourteen months of rain and snow had gone over that place, and there was still enough in the earth for a horse to find it.

“What is it?” Addai said again.

“Salt,” Orozbai said.

He went back to the fire and sat down and did not say anything else, and after a while Addai stopped asking. Orozbai sat looking into the fire and listening to the horses’ tongues on the frozen ground behind him, a small dry rasping sound in the dark, very patient, going on and on, long after the boy was asleep.

⁂

[Translator’s note: the passage from “He knew then where they were” to the end of the chapter does not appear in the 1962 edition, which ends the chapter at the camp below the pass. It is restored from the author’s typescript. Beside it the censor has written “excesses are not typical”. He let the same story stand in the next chapter, where a commissar hears it and is ashamed, and cut it here, where nobody is. Under his note, in Satarov’s hand, is a single line: the price of a pound of salt in Namangan in the winter of 1921.]
